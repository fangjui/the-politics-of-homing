01 The home
as engine

The home is not where politics happens to you. It is where you are made political — or not.

The modern home is a political settlement disguised as private life.

We do not primarily face a housing crisis. We face a crisis of the institutional form through which dwelling has been organised — and an intimate expression of the deeper crisis of the world we continue to make and unmake.

The dominant modern house encodes a particular theory of the self: separated, self-contained, ownable, mortgageable. Its walls do not merely divide inside from outside. They teach, every day, that the household is an autonomous unit — that one's dependencies are elsewhere, optional, and contracted-in. This is not architecture. It is ontology in brick and title.

Separation defines the unit. Dominion establishes control over it. Extraction converts its relations into yield. Financialisation optimises and capitalises that yield. These four logics are not four separate pathologies. They are one recursive apparatus — and the house is the coupling device through which they reinforce one another across generations.

Four logics that constitute a single apparatus. Asset value (financialisation) reinforces the boundary (separation) and the rights of dominion. The circuit is self-sustaining.

This is not a claim that every house is a prison. Home remains a genuine site of refuge, intimacy, memory, and autonomy. The crisis is that these real goods have become entangled with an institutional architecture that simultaneously produces debt, time poverty, gendered labour, ecological extraction, and social separation. The goods are real. So is the apparatus.

02 How the home
was mined

We mined the home to build the economy. The home bore the cost. Civil society paid the price.

The financialised economy was not built from nothing. It was built by extracting time, care, and capacity from the household economy.

Through mortgages, we converted future household labour into present capital. Through the double-income household, we drew both adults into full-time wage dependency — not to expand household freedom, but to meet housing costs whose rise was partly produced by that same expanded borrowing capacity. Time poverty was not an accident. It was a recursive outcome.

The extraction circuit. Each stage creates the conditions for the next. Civil society bears the residual: time stripped from its base unit leaves associations unable to sustain themselves.

The household economy is foundational. It produces viability: people capable of entering the world with care, language, confidence, and emotional coherence. Markets depend upon it while refusing to count it. The result is a structural contradiction: the household remains indispensable, but its capacity to perform its core functions has been steadily weakened.

The home became the reason for working and the place one has progressively less time to inhabit.

The household was not simply neglected. It was mined. More wage dependency, more debt, more housing extraction, more outsourcing, more pressure on time: all of this was loaded onto households as though they could absorb it indefinitely. Care was first privatised into the household and then commodified as households were compelled to buy back the capacities that time poverty had eroded.

This argument has a lineage. Silvia Federici's work — and a generation of feminist scholars from Brazil, India, and across the majority world who have extended it — established that reproductive labour was not a side-effect of capitalism but its hidden subsidy. The household was the site through which surplus was extracted before any factory gate was reached. Women's unwaged domestic labour kept the price of labour artificially low. The home was mined first, and most thoroughly, through this expropriation. The politics of homing is built on this foundation: any account of civic power that does not begin with reproductive labour has already accepted a false starting point.

03 What we lost

When the home loses capacity, civil society loses its base unit.

Neighbourlessness is not an emotional condition. It is a loss of operational capacity.

When care, mobility, tools, energy, recreation, and spare capacity are privately duplicated inside each self-contained household, practical reliance on neighbours declines. Shared facilities and repeated encounters become less structurally necessary. As shared capacity weakens, private provision appears still more necessary. The circuit closes.

The house-form removes the need to neighbour; the housing-cost regime removes the time. These two mechanisms are distinct. They require different remedies. Both must be named.

Fewer relationships through which care can circulate, vulnerability can be recognised, resources can be pooled, risks can be sensed, and common action can be undertaken. This is what the Power Book calls the composition deficit — but it begins before any registry or protocol. It begins in the home.

Power Construction identifies the substrate layer of civic power — the material and relational base from which participation is possible. The home is that substrate's first stratum. Where households are exhausted by rent, debt, precarious work, and depleted care, formal freedom of association becomes thin. A person without spare time cannot contribute reliably. A household without reserves cannot take risks.

Home capacity stripped → Time poverty → Neighbouring declines → Association weakens → Composition deficit

This is why the rebuilding of household capacity is more than compensatory social policy. It is investment in civil society's power to act. The composition deficit described in Power Construction is partly downstream of the household deficit described here.

04 From home
to homing

Civil society is amorphous. Homing is not.

Everyone has some idea of a home. Homing gives the politics of civil society a visceral, universal, economically grounded anchor it has never had.

The word "civil society" has a fundamental problem: it is nobody's lived experience. It names an abstraction. When we try to organise around it, we find ourselves explaining what it is before we can argue for why it matters. People get stuck on civil society organisations rather than citizenry; on structures rather than the felt conditions of life together.

The move from home to homing is not decorative. Home is a noun — a fixed thing to defend. Homing is a verb — a practice of belonging, at multiple scales simultaneously. A person is always homing: sustaining the conditions in which life can be reproduced, in a dwelling, in a neighbourhood, in a commons, in a political body.

The neighbourhood scale is not a ring drawn around the household — it is a field of gravitational pull organised around attractors: the primary school, the shared park, the recurring encounter. Its edge is where that pull weakens, not where an administrator drew a line. Household and body are bounded. The neighbourhood is fuzzy. The commons and civic body are constituted through protocol and law.

This distinction matters politically. A bounded neighbourhood can be administered from above — allocated to a councillor, assigned to a local authority, served. An attractor neighbourhood must be constituted from within. It has no edge that an outsider can draw. Simon Duffy and Tom French's mapping of Sheffield's 147 neighbourhoods — each around 4,000 people, each drawn by residents rather than officials — is an empirical demonstration of this: when asked to draw their own neighbourhood, people drew attractor fields, not administrative zones.

This also dissolves the false boundary of civil society. The pub is part of homing. The neighbourhood kitchen is part of homing. The allotment, the community trust, the street — all of these are sites where homing is practised. The politics of homing can include them without definitional gymnastics.

The prefix eco — in ecology, economics, ecosystem — comes from the Greek oikos: the home. It was always supposed to be a politics of balance and care. It never was. The politics of homing is the attempt to make that etymology real.

05 A politics
of homing

A new homing politics gives power its hard edge — and its human scale.

Where Power Construction asks how distributed agency becomes consequential capacity, the politics of homing asks what kind of life must be possible before that question can be answered at all.

The two books share a common substrate layer. Power Construction identifies the home as the first stratum of civic capacity: the source of time, security, reserves, care, learning, and digital access from which participation flows. Without that stratum in reasonable condition, the whole architecture above it is aspirational fiction.

The politics of homing develops what Power Construction only names. It argues that reconstituting the household as a site of care, capacity, and civic formation is not compensatory welfare. It is the precondition of any meaningful theory of collective power.

Power grows through proof, Power Construction argues. But proof requires participants. Participants require time. Time requires a household that is not entirely consumed by its own reproduction. The home is not the end of politics. It is its substrate.

The politics of homing is also a theory of authority. Who has the right to decide what a home is for? The dominant answer — the mortgage lender, the planner, the market — has produced separation, dominion, extraction, and financialisation. A homing politics argues for a different constitutional order of the dwelling: one in which the conditions of life together — care, time, neighbouring, ecological relation — have standing alongside financial return.

This connects directly to Power Construction's constitutional substrate: the commitment that civic power must be constituted and bounded through accountable, corrigible protocols. The home is where that commitment either has material grounding or does not. An abstract right of association means little to someone working two jobs to cover housing costs in a city that has financialised every square metre.

06 The constructive
turn

The task is not to restore the home. It is to reconstitute it on different terms.

Distinction without severance. Privacy without the denial of interdependence. Security without systematic exclusion. Investment without single-capital sovereignty.

The next household economy is neither pure privatisation nor total collectivisation. It is a layered system in which household autonomy is strengthened precisely because some of the burdens currently crushing it are redistributed into more durable and shared infrastructures.

Once assets are shared, the burden on each individual household declines. Once care is shared, time pressure can be lowered. Once some forms of provisioning are held collectively, the household no longer has to purchase every missing function through market income. This creates the possibility of rebuilding slack within everyday life — the temporal and relational surplus from which civic participation, democratic capacity, and common action become possible again.

It opens with possibility — with a recognisable but different way of living — and then works backwards to show what would make it structurally possible. This is the same argumentative structure as Power Construction.

07 What would
falsify this

A working argument owes its failure conditions.

F1 The substrate claim fails. If consequential civic participation is thriving in households that are exhausted, financially precarious, and time-poor — if the composition deficit cannot be shown to depend on household capacity — the argument that the home is the substrate of power loses its force.
F2 The universality claim fails. If "homing" does not in practice feel more universal than "civil society" — if it elicits the same abstraction, the same CSO-think, the same disconnection from lived experience — the rhetorical wager of this book is lost.
F3 The ontological claim fails. If the separative ontology of the modern home is not taught through daily inhabitation — if people who live in self-contained, financialised dwellings do not disproportionately exhibit separative political dispositions — the naturalisation channel described in Section 01 does not hold.
F4 The reconstruction claim fails. If, where genuinely shared provisioning is available and well-designed — tool libraries, common kitchens, shared energy, co-housing — households still maintain full private duplication, then the drive to separate is a preference, not a structural compulsion, and the constructive argument weakens to taste.
F5 The political claim fails. If the politics of homing cannot cross the lines of tenure, class, and housing form — if it speaks only to property-owning anxieties about protection — it reproduces the incumbency channel rather than countering it.

None of these falsifiers is currently met on available evidence. But the argument stands under them, not above them. The Heart Power portfolio provides one test: the interventions across survival, mobilisation, and legal infrastructure either build the substrate the politics of homing claims is necessary — or they do not.
